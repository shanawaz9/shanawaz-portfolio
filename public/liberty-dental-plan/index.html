01Discovery & Research

Turning a complex healthcare application
into a simpler experience

A decade-old application serving members, providers and brokers, with navigation scattered across the product. The client wanted it responsive. New to US healthcare, we learned the domain first.

Discovery ran on one shared FigJam board, with the team in it at the same time: gatherings, research, competitive analysis, personas, journeys. Working in one place kept the process intact when the timeline tightened.

We conducted:

  • US healthcare market research
  • Competitive analysis (6) Delta Dental, DentaQuest, UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, Cigna, MCNA Dental
  • Existing product analysis
  • Client discussions and requirement gathering
  • Personas for the three audiences
  • User journey and task flow mapping
  • Design workshops and brainstorming

What the rest of the market was already doing

I reviewed the six for the patterns that repeat: navigation, provider search, member self-service, accessibility, personalization and mobile. Not to copy anyone, but to find where LDP could simplify.

The real problem

From a design lead perspective, the first challenge was clear: how do we structure the information and navigation around three different personas without making the product fragmented?

Navigation was not a menu problem. It was an information architecture problem.

02IA & User Flows

Build the right foundation before designing the interface

Instead of jumping into visual design, we solved the structure first, and put three approaches in front of the client.

Option 01
Persona-based

Each persona sees only what is relevant to them, with dedicated local navigation underneath.

Approved
Option 02
Category-based

One persistent header opening a full-width mega menu that groups every category into configurable columns.

Not taken
Option 03
Search-based

An “I am a…” selector with guided search as the main route into plans, quotes and provider services.

Not taken

Navigation benchmark, from the competitive review

Navigation modelAdoption among competitorsUser satisfaction (est.)Fit for Liberty Dental
Persona-based ~65% ~82% Strong fit for multiple user types: members, providers and brokers
Mega menu ~35% ~68% Usable on desktop, but clutters on mobile

Adoption was counted across the six competitors. Satisfaction is an estimate from that review, not a measured result from this project.

The persona-based approach emerged as the strongest direction, and the client agreed to move forward with it.

Research, workshops and repeated discussions narrowed the field. The decision itself came out of one exercise.

Card sorting, on a table, with paper

My decision

We ran card sorting inside the UX team, every page and tool on its own card, placed by what the client wanted surfaced against how the three personas actually look for information.

Where those disagreed we argued the card out, then took a position to the client rather than a question. That set the priority order: what earns primary navigation, what sits a level down, what had never earned its place.

We ran it physically rather than in a digital tool. Paper on a table meant everyone reached in and moved something, so disagreements surfaced in an afternoon instead of as comments on a board a week later.

We then translated the information architecture into a full sitemap, page by page.

03Process & Constraints

The timeline started fighting the process

Once the structure came together, the project became a balance of UX quality, business expectations and a tight timeline. Developers pushed to start while UX was still moving, and wireframes were read as finished design.

Can we just start development with the wireframes and add the visual design later?

As the offshore design lead I pushed back. The requirements and designs were still changing, and starting early would have bought a few weeks at the cost of rework.

The conflict

Project managers and developers wanted to start building.

The risk

Requirements and wireframes were still moving. Starting early bought weeks and cost rework.

My response

I negotiated the timeline instead of removing UX steps, and kept the sequence intact.

WireframesApprovalResponsiveVisual designHandoff
Outcome

A structured process, with the UX work still done in the right order.

Instead of removing UX steps to meet the timeline, we negotiated the process.

Copy became part of the design process

Stakeholder constraint

Because this was an insurance product, the client wanted copy settled before design. In practice the two ran in parallel, and copy changes kept moving the wireframes.

04Responsive Navigation

We stopped trying to solve everything at once

Instead of letting changing requirements dictate the process, we separated the work into clear stages and kept iterating with the client.

The navigation challenge

Navigation was one of the biggest UX challenges.

The desktop structure ran from the landing page, into a persona-based flow, then local navigation, then three subcategories.

The challenge was translating that into a responsive mobile experience. We explored three.

My decision

Rather than defending the first direction, I used the rejections to understand how the client was thinking, and carried that into the next pass.

We also narrowed down long navigational pages and explored more efficient progress bars, forms, and contextual information.

Developer constraint

Bootstrap components were another constraint. With the developers, we customized them where usability needed it. Responsive design was built alongside the core experience, not bolted on after.

05Visual Design & UI

From structure to a scalable visual system

Once the wireframes were approved, we moved into visual design.

The focus was on:

Typography Color system Components Micro-interactions Visual hierarchy Accessibility Responsive behavior

Three visual directions

Before settling the system, we built the same homepage three times so the client could compare like with like rather than react to adjectives.

What actually changed

The whole redesign is legible in the first screen of the homepage, so it is worth putting the two side by side.

The shipped direction leads with people. A photograph of three generations answers who this is for before a word is read, on a page a member often reaches while worried about a claim.

The faces are the engagement mechanism, not the ornament.

The rebrand made that possible. Purple had coated every surface, so it carried no information. The new palette sets a deep navy against quiet warm neutrals and holds one blue back for action.

Design trade-off

The top of the page is deliberately marketing-led: few surfaces, large imagery, short copy. The density is saved for the screens where members do the work.

Typography

We had to balance SEO requirements, readability, and visual hierarchy.

Color

We aligned the color system with the client's brand guidelines. The primary brand color also changed during the project, so we explored and adapted the system accordingly.

Accessibility decisions

Accessibility was handled inside the system rather than as a pass at the end. These are the decisions it produced, not an audit.

Color contrastEvery token tied to a named role, so text, action, surface and border colors are chosen by the usage table rather than by eye.
TypographyOne type scale across desktop, tablet and mobile, with line height and weight fixed per style.
Action clarityA firm visual split between primary and secondary actions, carried through the component set.
Responsive behaviorBuilt alongside the core experience, so small screens get the same structure rather than a reduced one.
06Outcome & Handoff

A complex product delivered through iteration and collaboration

We moved from a legacy application with scattered information to a structured, persona-based experience, after multiple rounds of exploration, rejection, client feedback and approval.

What shipped

  • Persona-based navigation, approved and running in production
  • Responsive navigation across desktop and mobile, with the drill-down menu on small screens
  • A restructured information architecture and a full page-by-page sitemap
  • A type scale and color system with every token tied to a named role
  • More efficient progress bars, forms and contextual information on long pages
  • UX reviews continued through production, not stopped at handoff

★Reflection & Learnings

What I learned

This project was less about drawing screens and more about leading design through uncertainty.

The balance that ran through all of it: user needs, business requirements, technical constraints and deadlines.

Conflicts came up around IA, navigation, copy, development pressure, responsive behavior and accessibility. Treated as inputs rather than blockers, each one sharpened the solution.

As Senior UX Designer and Offshore Design Lead, it pushed my leadership, stakeholder management and design-management as much as my craft.

A roller coaster, but a useful one.

Shanawaz, Product Designer
