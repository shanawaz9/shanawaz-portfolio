Rebuilding the UX foundation of a decade-old healthcare platform

Years of added content and requirements had left Liberty Dental Plan hard to navigate for members, providers and brokers. I owned the architecture, the navigation and the design direction, through to production.

The change, in one screen

01  The Challenge

The product had grown faster than its structure

A decade of additions had piled up. Navigation was scattered across the product, and one screen could offer three different routes to the same plan.

It served members, providers and brokers, three audiences with little in common, through one generic structure. The client also wanted the whole thing responsive.

The interface did not just look dated. The structure no longer matched how people actually used the product.

02  Discovery & Research

None of us had worked in US dental insurance before

So I put the team on the domain before anyone opened a design file. Getting the plan types wrong would have produced a confident structure built on the wrong model.

Discovery ran on one shared FigJam board, with the team in it at the same time: gatherings, research, competitive analysis, personas, journeys. Working in one place kept the process intact when the timeline tightened.

We conducted:

  • US healthcare market research
  • Competitive analysis (6) Delta Dental, DentaQuest, UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, Cigna, MCNA Dental
  • Existing product analysis
  • Client workshops and requirement gathering
  • Personas for the three audiences
  • User journey and task flow mapping

What the rest of the market was already doing

I reviewed the six for the patterns that repeat: navigation, member self-service, accessibility, personalization and mobile. Not to copy anyone, but to find where LDP could simplify.

AreaKey observationLDP opportunity
Navigation Complex and scattered across several of the experiences Simplify the information architecture
Member experience Claims, benefits and tools often take several steps Reduce friction in the key tasks
Accessibility WCAG is increasingly emphasized Build accessibility into the experience
Personalization Some competitors tailor content more strongly Make content more relevant to members
Mobile Quality varies widely Prioritize responsive, mobile-first journeys

What the research changed

Scattered navigation

The same content sat in more than one menu, so no single route could be made the right one.

Design implication Rework the information architecture before redesigning any screen.
Three audiences, one structure

Members, providers and brokers all entered through the same navigation, which mixed audiences with products.

Design implication Split the structure by audience rather than by content type.
Mobile as an afterthought

Across competitors, mobile quality varied widely, and the legacy menus did not survive a small screen.

Design implication Design the responsive pattern deliberately instead of adapting the desktop one.

The real problem

From a design lead perspective, the first challenge was clear: how do we structure the information and navigation around three different personas without making the product fragmented?

Navigation was not a menu problem. It was an information architecture problem.

03  Finding the Structure

We could not redesign the navigation until we knew what belonged together

My decision

I ran card sorting inside the UX team, every page and tool on its own card, placed by what the client wanted surfaced against how the three personas actually look for information.

Where those disagreed we argued the card out, then took a position to the client rather than a question. That set the priority order: what earns primary navigation, what sits a level down, what had never earned its place.

We ran it physically rather than in a digital tool. Paper on a table meant everyone reached in and moved something, so disagreements surfaced in an afternoon instead of as comments on a board a week later.
04  Information Architecture

Three directions for the structure

The card sort gave us content groupings, but not a navigation model. With three audiences to serve, members, providers and brokers, I put three directions to the client at the same wireframe fidelity.

Direction A
Category-based

Solved Easy access to deep content.

Trade-off Became dense, and harder to adapt for mobile.

Not selected
Direction B
Search-based

Solved Guided users toward specific tasks.

Trade-off Less effective for exploration.

Not selected
Direction C
Persona-based

Solved Clear pathways for members, providers and brokers.

Trade-off More complexity in the underlying IA.

Selected

Why persona-based?

It matched the platform's three distinct audiences and followed recognition over recall: people could start from their own context instead of learning Liberty's internal content structure.

The client agreed to move forward with this direction.

I then translated the architecture into a full sitemap, page by page.

05  The Real Design Challenge

Keeping UX moving while everything around it changed

The hard part was not the interface. It was holding the structure steady while requirements, copy, the brand color and the delivery date all moved underneath it.

Can we just start development with the wireframes and add the visual design later?

Development wanted to start early
ConstraintProject managers and developers wanted to begin building while the UX work was still moving.
RiskWireframes were being read as finished design. Building against unstable UX would have bought a few weeks and cost rework.
My responseAs offshore design lead I held the sequence, and made the case for it to the people pushing against it.
ResultThe process stayed intact: wireframes, approval, responsive, visual design, handoff.
Copy had to be settled before design
ConstraintBecause this is an insurance product, the client wanted copy approved ahead of the design.
RiskIn practice the two ran in parallel, and every copy change moved the wireframes again.
My responseI kept wireframes and visual direction progressing alongside the copy rather than waiting on it.
ResultCopy became part of the design process instead of a gate in front of it.
The build was tied to Bootstrap
ConstraintImplementation was based on Bootstrap components, which set limits on what the UI could do.
RiskDesigning past the component set would have produced screens the team could not build as drawn.
My responseI worked with the developers to customize components only where usability needed it.
ResultResponsive behavior was built alongside the core experience rather than bolted on after.

Instead of removing UX steps to meet the timeline, we negotiated the process.

06  Navigation & Responsive

Three navigation directions. One final pattern.

The desktop structure ran from the landing page, into a persona-based flow, then local navigation, then three subcategories.

Translating that into mobile was the hardest single problem on the project.

My decision

Rather than defending the first direction, I used the rejections to understand how the client was thinking, and carried that into the next pass.

How the pattern translates

Desktop
PersonaLocal navSubcategory

All three levels stay visible at once, with a breadcrumb back to the persona board.

Mobile
PersonaLocal navSubcategory

The same three levels, one at a time. Choosing a persona slides to its own panel with a back arrow.

I also narrowed the long navigational pages and explored more efficient progress bars, forms, and contextual information.

07  Visual Design

Three homepages before one system

With the wireframes approved, we moved into visual design. Before settling anything, we built the same homepage three times so the client could compare like with like rather than react to adjectives.

The shipped direction leads with people. A photograph of three generations answers who this is for before a word is read, on a page a member often reaches while worried about a claim.

The rebrand made that possible. Purple had coated every surface, so it carried no information. The new palette sets a deep navy against quiet warm neutrals and holds one blue back for action.

Design trade-off

The top of the page is deliberately marketing-led: few surfaces, large imagery, short copy. The density is saved for the screens where members do the work.

Typography

We had to balance SEO requirements, readability, and visual hierarchy.

Color

I aligned the color system with the client's brand guidelines. The primary brand color changed mid-project, so we explored and adapted the system around it.

Accessibility decisions

Accessibility was handled inside the system rather than as a pass at the end. These are the decisions it produced, not an audit.

Color contrastEvery token tied to a named role, so text, action, surface and border colors are chosen by the usage table rather than by eye.
TypographySizes and line heights set per breakpoint, so body copy stays readable on a phone instead of shrinking to fit.
Action clarityA firm visual split between primary and secondary actions, carried through the component set.
Responsive behaviorBuilt alongside the core experience, so small screens get the same structure rather than a reduced one.
08  What Shipped

Persona-based navigation, live in production

It took several rounds of exploration, rejection and client feedback to get here.

What shipped

  • Persona-based navigation, approved and running in production
  • Responsive navigation across desktop and mobile, with the drill-down menu on small screens
  • A restructured information architecture and a full page-by-page sitemap
  • A type scale and color system with every token tied to a named role
  • More efficient progress bars, forms and contextual information on long pages
  • UX reviews continued through production, not stopped at handoff

—  Reflection

What I took from it

Settle the structure early, and say the reasoning out loud. Most of the arguments on this project were winnable only because the card sort had already answered them.

The balance that ran through all of it: user needs, business requirements, technical constraints and deadlines.

As Senior UX Designer and Offshore Design Lead, it pushed my leadership, stakeholder management and design-management as much as my craft.

A roller coaster, but a useful one.
