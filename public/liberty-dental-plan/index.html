01Discovery & Research

Turning a complex healthcare application into a simpler experience

We started with an existing healthcare application that had been around for a long time. Information and navigation were scattered across the product, making the experience difficult to structure.

The application served three groups of users: members, providers and brokers. The client also wanted a mobile-responsive experience.

Since we were new to US healthcare, we first focused on understanding the domain before jumping into design.

We conducted:

  • US healthcare market research
  • Competitive analysis
  • Existing product analysis
  • Client discussions and requirement gathering
  • Design workshops and brainstorming
From a design lead perspective, the first challenge was clear: how do we structure the information and navigation around three different personas without making the product fragmented?
02IA & User Flows

Build the right foundation before designing the interface

Instead of immediately jumping into visual design, we wanted to solve the structure of the product first.

We explored three approaches:

  1. Persona-based. Each persona sees the information relevant to them, with dedicated local navigation.
  2. Category-based. Information is organized through configurable categories.
  3. Search-based. Users rely more on search and advanced search to find information.

We used card sorting, research, client workshops, and multiple design discussions to narrow down the direction.

The persona-based approach emerged as the strongest direction, and the client agreed to move forward with it.

We then translated the information architecture into page-to-page user flows.

03Process & Constraints

The timeline started fighting the process

Once the structure started coming together, the project became a balance between UX quality, business expectations, and a tight development timeline.

The project managers and developers were pushing toward development while the UX work was still evolving. There was also a misunderstanding around the difference between wireframes and final visual design.

The pressure was essentially:

Can we just start development with the wireframes and add the visual design later?

From my perspective as the offshore design lead, I pushed back because the requirements and designs were still changing. Starting development too early would create rework and potentially affect the quality of the product.

Instead of removing UX steps, we negotiated the timeline and kept the process: wireframes, approval, responsive design, visual design.

But that wasn't the only conflict.

Copy became part of the design process

Because this was an insurance/healthcare product, the client prioritized copywriting before design.

While copywriting was being finalized, we were also progressing with wireframes and visual direction. Changes to the copy sometimes affected the design.

At the same time, we were exploring multiple visual directions in parallel.

04Responsive Navigation

We stopped trying to solve everything at once

Instead of letting changing requirements dictate the process, we separated the work into clear stages and kept iterating with the client.

Navigation

Navigation was one of the biggest UX challenges.

The desktop structure ran from the landing page, into a persona-based flow, then local navigation, then three subcategories.

The challenge was translating this into a responsive mobile experience.

We explored three approaches. The first two were rejected.

Instead of defending the original direction, we used the feedback to understand how the client was thinking. The third, a drill-down menu, was the one approved.

The final approach was approved.

Simplifying the experience

We also narrowed down long navigational pages and explored more efficient progress bars, forms, and contextual information.

Bootstrap components were another constraint. With support from the developers, we customized them where usability needed it. Responsive design was built alongside the core experience rather than treated as an afterthought.

05Visual Design & UI

From structure to a scalable visual system

Once the wireframes were approved, we moved into visual design.

The focus was on:

Typography Color system Components Micro-interactions Visual hierarchy Accessibility Responsive behavior

Typography

We had to balance SEO requirements, readability, and visual hierarchy.

Color

We aligned the color system with the client's brand guidelines. The primary brand color also changed during the project, so we explored and adapted the system accordingly.

06Outcome & Handoff

A complex product delivered through iteration and collaboration

The final design went through multiple rounds of research, exploration, rejection, client feedback, refinement, and approval.

We moved from a legacy application with scattered information toward a more structured, persona-based experience with clearer navigation and responsive behavior.

★Reflection & Learnings

What I learned

This project was less about creating individual screens and more about leading design through uncertainty.

The biggest learning was balancing:

User needs, business requirements, technical constraints and deadlines.

There were conflicts around information architecture, navigation, copywriting, development pressure, responsive design, and accessibility.

Instead of treating these as blockers, we used them to refine the solution.

As a Senior UX Designer and Offshore Design Lead, the project also pushed me to improve my leadership, collaboration, stakeholder management, and design-management skills.

A roller coaster, but a useful one.
