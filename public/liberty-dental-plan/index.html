Before and after

01  The Problem

The product had grown faster than its structure

Liberty Dental Plan serves members, providers and brokers, three audiences with little in common. All three came in through one generic structure that mixed audiences with products.

A decade of additions had piled up on top of it. Navigation was scattered across the product, and one screen could offer three different routes to the same plan. The client also wanted the whole platform responsive, and the legacy menus did not survive a small screen.

What was wrong
Information was hard to find, the same content lived in several places, and there was no clear home for anything new.
Who it hit
Members, providers and brokers. Three groups with different goals, sharing one way in.
Why it was hard
A US dental insurance domain none of us knew, copy that needed approval, a Bootstrap-based build, and a brand that changed partway through.
What I had to solve
A structure that gives each audience a clear path, works on a phone, and can take new content without falling apart again.

The interface did not just look dated. The structure no longer matched how people actually used the product.

02  Understanding the Context

None of us had worked in US dental insurance before

So I put the team on the domain before anyone opened a design file. Getting the plan types wrong would have produced a confident structure built on the wrong model.

Discovery ran on one shared FigJam board, with the team in it at the same time: gatherings, research, competitive analysis, personas, journeys. Working in one place kept the process intact when the timeline tightened.

What each piece of research was for:

  • US healthcare market research How plans, members, providers and brokers relate, so the structure started from the right model.
  • Competitive analysis (6) Delta Dental, DentaQuest, UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, Cigna, MCNA Dental. Which patterns the market repeats, and where LDP could be simpler.
  • Existing product analysis Where the same content lived in more than one menu, and why no single route felt like the right one.
  • Client workshops and requirement gathering What the business needed surfaced, and the limits we would be working within.
  • Personas for the three audiences Members, providers and brokers look for information differently. One shared structure would always be wrong for someone.
  • User journey and task flow mapping How each audience moves through the product. This fed the priority order we later set in the card sort.

What the rest of the market was already doing

I reviewed the six for the patterns that repeat: navigation, member self-service, accessibility, personalization and mobile. Not to copy anyone, but to find where LDP could simplify.

AreaKey observationLDP opportunity
Navigation Complex and scattered across several of the experiences Simplify the information architecture
Member experience Claims, benefits and tools often take several steps Reduce friction in the key tasks
Accessibility WCAG is increasingly emphasized Build accessibility into the experience
Personalization Some competitors tailor content more strongly Make content more relevant to members
Mobile Quality varies widely Prioritize responsive, mobile-first journeys

What this meant for LDPThe market was not setting a high bar for navigation or mobile. Getting both right was the clearest way to stand apart.

03  Defining the Problems

Four problems worth solving

Research turned a redesign brief into four specific problems. Each one changed what we would design first.

  1. Different users, one way in Members, providers and brokers all entered through the same navigation, which mixed audiences with products. What it meant Split the structure by audience rather than by content type.
  2. Information was hard to find The same content sat in more than one menu, so no single route could be made the right one. What it meant Rework the information architecture before redesigning any screen.
  3. The structure could not grow A decade of additions had piled up with no structure to hold them. Adding more the same way would repeat the problem. What it meant Give every type of content a clear home, and build shared patterns new pages can reuse.
  4. Mobile was an afterthought The legacy menus did not survive a small screen, and mobile quality varied widely across competitors too. What it meant Design the responsive pattern deliberately instead of adapting the desktop one, with accessibility built in.

The question I had to answer

How do we structure the information and navigation around three different personas without making the product fragmented?

Navigation was not a menu problem. It was a product structure problem.

04  Restructuring the Product

Restructuring the product around how people actually use it

We could not redesign the navigation until we knew what belonged together, and for whom. So the first design work was structural, not visual.

Validating what belongs together

My decision

I ran card sorting inside the UX team, every page and tool on its own card, placed by what the client wanted surfaced against how the three personas actually look for information.

Where those disagreed we argued the card out, then took a position to the client rather than a question. That set the priority order: what earns primary navigation, what sits a level down, and what had never earned its place.

We ran it physically rather than in a digital tool. Paper on a table meant everyone reached in and moved something, so disagreements surfaced in an afternoon instead of as comments on a board a week later.

Choosing a structural model

The card sort gave us content groupings, but not a model for the product. With three audiences to serve, members, providers and brokers, I put three directions to the client at the same wireframe fidelity.

Problem
Three audiences with different goals, one body of content.
Options
Category-based, search-based or persona-based.
Decision
Persona-based, with content categories sitting under each audience.
Why
It matched the platform's three distinct audiences and followed recognition over recall: people start from their own context instead of learning Liberty's internal content structure. It also gives new content an obvious home, under the audience it serves.
Trade-off
A more complex IA underneath, which the sitemap had to carry.

The client agreed to move forward with this direction.

I then translated the architecture into a full sitemap, page by page. It became the reference for every screen that followed.

05  One Structure, Every Screen

Making the same structure work on a phone

Navigation was where the new structure met the user, so it had to carry the persona model on every screen size.

The desktop structure ran from the landing page, into a persona-based flow, then local navigation, then three subcategories.

Translating that into mobile was the hardest single problem on the project. The aim was the same structure on a small screen, not a reduced version of it.

My decision

Rather than defending the first direction, I used the rejections to understand how the client was thinking, and carried that into the next pass.

Problem
Three levels of navigation had to fit on a phone without losing the way back.
Options
A dropdown menu, an accordion menu, and a drill-down menu.
Decision
The drill-down menu.
Why
It shows one level at a time with a back arrow to the last, so mobile follows the same persona, local nav, subcategory path as desktop.

I also narrowed the long navigational pages and explored more efficient progress bars, forms, and contextual information.

06  Bringing It Together

Three homepages before one system

With the structure and wireframes approved, we moved into visual design. Before settling anything, we built the same homepage three times so the client could compare like with like rather than react to adjectives.

The shipped direction leads with people. A photograph of three generations answers who this is for before a word is read, on a page a member often reaches while worried about a claim.

The rebrand made that possible. Purple had coated every surface, so it carried no information. The new palette sets a deep navy against quiet warm neutrals and holds one blue back for action.

Design trade-off

The top of the page is deliberately marketing-led: few surfaces, large imagery, short copy. The density is saved for the screens where members do the work.

A system, not just a new look

The visual work mattered less as a style and more as the thing that would keep a large product consistent once we stepped away.

  • Consistency. One type scale and one set of color roles across every page and breakpoint.
  • Room to grow. A new page starts from decisions already made, not from a blank file.
  • Easier to build. Components stayed close to the Bootstrap set the build used, customized only where usability needed it.

Typography

We had to balance SEO requirements, readability, and visual hierarchy. One scale covers every breakpoint, so the hierarchy holds from desktop to phone.

Color

I aligned the color system with the client's brand guidelines. The primary brand color changed mid-project, so we explored and adapted the system around it.

Accessibility decisions

Accessibility was handled inside the system rather than as a pass at the end. These are the decisions it produced, not an audit.

Color contrastEvery token tied to a named role, so text, action, surface and border colors are chosen by the usage table rather than by eye.
TypographySizes and line heights set per breakpoint, so body copy stays readable on a phone instead of shrinking to fit.
Action clarityA firm visual split between primary and secondary actions, carried through the component set.
Responsive behaviorBuilt alongside the core experience, so small screens get the same structure rather than a reduced one.
07  Getting It Built

Keeping the design moving while everything around it changed

The hard part was not the interface. It was holding the structure steady while requirements, copy, the brand color and the delivery date all moved underneath it, and doing that with the people building it, not around them.

Can we just start development with the wireframes and add the visual design later?

Development wanted to start early
ConstraintProject managers and developers wanted to begin building while the design was still moving.
RiskWireframes were being read as finished design. Building against an unsettled design would have bought a few weeks and cost rework.
My responseAs offshore design lead I held the sequence, and made the case for it to the people pushing against it.
ResultThe process stayed intact: wireframes, approval, responsive, visual design, handoff.
Copy had to be settled before design
ConstraintBecause this is an insurance product, the client wanted copy approved ahead of the design.
RiskIn practice the two ran in parallel, and every copy change moved the wireframes again.
My responseI kept wireframes and visual direction progressing alongside the copy rather than waiting on it.
ResultCopy became part of the design process instead of a gate in front of it.
The build was tied to Bootstrap
ConstraintImplementation was based on Bootstrap components, which set limits on what the UI could do.
RiskDesigning past the component set would have produced screens the team could not build as drawn.
My responseI worked with the developers to customize components only where usability needed it.
ResultResponsive behavior was built alongside the core experience rather than bolted on after.

Instead of cutting design steps to meet the timeline, we negotiated the process.

Working with the developers

My role

Visual approval was not the end. We worked closely with developers during handoff to make sure what got built matched what was designed.

Design

Wireframes and visual design, approved stage by stage.

Handoff

Walked the developers through the intent, not just the files.

Component discussions

Bootstrap components customized together where usability needed it.

Implementation review

Design reviews against the build, with usability testing running alongside.

Iteration

Design reviews continued during production rather than ending at handoff.

08  Outcome

A restructured platform, live in production

It took several rounds of exploration, rejection and client feedback to get here. The result is one product organized around the people who use it, instead of around a decade of additions.

  • A restructured information architecture and a full page-by-page sitemap

·  Learnings

What I took from it

Settle the structure early, and say the reasoning out loud. Most of the arguments on this project were winnable only because the card sort had already answered them.

Constraints are part of the brief. Copy approvals, Bootstrap and a moving brand color shaped the product as much as the research did. Treating them as inputs, not interruptions, kept the work moving.

The balance that ran through all of it: user needs, business requirements, technical constraints and deadlines.

As Senior Product Designer and Offshore Design Lead, it pushed my leadership, stakeholder management and design management as much as my craft.

A roller coaster, but a useful one.
