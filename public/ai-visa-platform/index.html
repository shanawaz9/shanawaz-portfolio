Redesigning an AI-built visa platform before launch

The client asked for a UI polish. After the audit we agreed to fix the UX first: the structure, the user flows and the visual design.

RoleSenior UX Designer
ProductAI visa evaluation platform
AudienceVisa applicants & attorneys
PlatformDesktop-first
Timeline3 weeks design · 1 week build
Business goalMarketing & lead generation
ScopeUX strategy · Information architecture · Interaction design · Visual direction · Design reviews · Team coordination · Design-to-development
01

What the client asked for

The founder had built the MVP with Claude and wanted a UI polish before launch. But the site collects a person’s immigration details and passes them to an attorney. For a task like that, clear structure and trust matter more than styling.

My call

So I suggested we fix the UX first. Instead of asking for a bigger scope, I walked the founder through the exact screens where users would drop off. She agreed.

Our job was not to make the site look better. It was to help people understand a big decision.

02

The problems we found in the UX audit

We looked at competitor products, went through the current user flows, and had discovery calls with the founder. The problems were not visual. They were in the structure and the flows.

We could not remove the content, so the question became how to group it.

03

The UX strategy, in three parts

  1. 01 Framework and information architecture

    Split one long page into six, with one job per page.

  2. 02 Visual characterisation

    Give the product its own visual identity, so it reads better and feels reliable.

  3. 03 Component efficiency

    Use components that hold the same content in less space.

Chapter 04

How we solved it

The three parts of the strategy, in the order we worked on them: information architecture and the user journey, then the visual direction, then the components.

4a

Information architecture and the conversion journey: one long page became six

The biggest change was the information architecture. Instead of one long scroll, we split the content into six pages and grouped related sections where users would look for them.

The AIDA + Trust framework, mapped to the user journey

The business goal was lead generation, so the site had to guide users to an evaluation or a consultation. We looked at a few frameworks and picked this one.

4b

Visual direction: giving the product its own identity

The new structure fixed the content problem, but the UI still looked generic.

So we worked on the visual design, section by section. Not a rebrand: just a clearer identity for the product that was already there.

It had to look like a product people could trust, not an AI template.

4c

Components that hold more content in less space

The content had to stay, so we needed components that could hold more of it in less space. These are the patterns we used:

Progressive disclosure Stacked content Secondary navigation Information merging Interactive components Stronger hierarchy Micro-interactions

The goal was not to hide information. It was to make it easier to scan.

05

Design and development stayed connected

I connected Claude Code to the Figma MCP and kept the Figma file in auto layout, so the build could follow the design closely. The developers stayed in the file, and we fixed UX and micro-interaction issues directly in code.

The tools matter less than the habit: keeping design and development in the same loop.

06

The result: a much clearer product

The product launched recently, so I do not have usage numbers yet. But the structural changes are easy to see.

·

The takeaway

Simplifying does not always mean removing content. Most of the time it means deciding where each piece belongs and how much attention it should get.

The request was a UI polish. The real opportunity was to fix the experience underneath.

Sometimes the most useful thing a designer can do is check whether the given problem is the real problem.
