Discover

Framing the problem, sizing the market, and deciding what to build first.

01 — The Problem

AI is changing the job market
faster than people can adapt.

New skills are becoming essential, but most professionals aren't learning or adapting to them. We saw an opportunity to help people understand:

  • How AI impacts their career
  • Where their skill gaps exist
  • What they should learn next
  • How they can transition into AI-driven roles

Can we build a product — Shiftlyn — that helps people understand their AI readiness and guides them toward the right upskilling path?

04 — Learning From Existing Products

Study what already works,
then earn the paywall.

Rather than guess at what makes someone trust an assessment — or pay for one — we took apart products that had already solved a version of this problem. Duolingo for habit and engagement, 16Personalities for personalized assessments and a results experience people actually forward to their friends.

Both were read through the same six lenses, so the exercise stayed a teardown rather than a list of features we happened to like.

05 — Discovering the Core Product

The results page
is the product.

The assessment simply collected data. The results page is where the user actually experiences Shiftlyn.

Designing for emotion

This is also the moment users are most emotionally invested — anxious about what the score will say, and looking for reassurance as much as information. So the results page couldn't just present data; it had to be designed around how someone feels reading it.

We designed it as an emotional arc: Anxiety → Understanding → Acceptance → Action. Reassuring language, a calm color system, and a clear next step turned what could feel like a verdict into guidance.

shiftlyn.com/results
  • 1
    Anxiety

    The score lands first — most users arrive uncertain about what it means for them.

  • 2
    Understanding

    Context follows immediately: plain-language reasons for why the score landed where it did.

  • 3
    Acceptance

    An "in your favour" highlight reframes the score as workable, not final.

  • 4
    Action

    Ranked, personalized next steps — the score never sits there with nowhere to go.

06 — The Dashboard

What happens after
the report is read.

A report is a one-time event. Once someone has read theirs, the product has no claim on them — which is the entire retention problem in one sentence.

The dashboard is the answer we built: a place where the result keeps changing because the world around it does. It opens on the user's current risk, how it moved since the last check, and how far along their skills are.

shiftlyn.com/dashboard
Dashboard — Risk SnapshotCurrent risk, movement since the last check, skills completed, and every past assessment kept on record.

Designed as return loops, not features

Each surface exists to answer a different version of "why open this again?" — and each one changes on its own schedule, so the answer stays true after the first week.

Communities Curated Discord, Slack and Reddit groups in the user's field. The pull is other people, so it doesn't depend on us shipping something.
Events Dated things to show up for. A calendar creates a reason to return that a static report never can.
Skill tracking 0 of 6 complete, visible on arrival. An unfinished bar is a standing invitation to finish it.
Job application tracking Where each application stands, kept beside the risk score that motivated it. The job hunt outlasts the assessment by months.
Plan & payment details Receipts and reports stay in one place. Users who can find what they bought are the ones who buy again.
Re-run the report Learn a skill or change roles, run it again, and watch the score move — the +25 pts card is the whole loop in one number.
Dashboard — Retention LoopsSix reasons to return, each running on a different clock: people, dates, progress, applications, records, and re-measurement.
Community & eventsIndia-focused groups with member counts and a direct join, split from a dated events tab.
Live India radarAI disruption news by sector, tagged by impact and sourced — the reason the risk score is never final.
CoursesMatched to the skill gaps in the report, split free and paid, with a best-match flag on the top result.
Dashboard SurfacesThree of the six loops in the product: people to join, signal to track, and a way to close the gaps the report found.

08 — Designing for Trust and Conversion

Marketing landing page:
trust before action.

AI career displacement is an emotionally charged topic. Users arrive with uncertainty about how AI might affect their future. The challenge wasn't just getting users to start the assessment — it was building enough trust for them to believe the results and take action.

To solve this, I designed the landing page using the C-TRUST-A Framework, a structure that guides users from awareness to action by progressively building clarity, credibility, and confidence.

shiftlyn.com
  • C
    Clarity

    One headline, one question, one next step — no ambiguity about what this product does.

  • T
    Transparency

    Time, cost, and data use are stated upfront — "2 minutes, no signup."

  • R
    Reliability

    Citing WEF and McKinsey research signals this isn't guesswork.

  • U
    Understandability

    A simple three-step structure replaces jargon with plain language.

  • S
    Social proof

    Real quotes from real roles, not generic praise.

  • T
    Trust

    An FAQ answers the hesitations users haven't said out loud yet.

  • A
    Action

    One final, low-friction CTA — the only decision left to make.

09 — Results Page Evolution

Three passes at
the same screen.

Because the results page carried the whole product, it got rewritten more than anything else we built. Three versions matter: the free state a user sees before paying, the first full report, and the report as it stands now.

The data behind them barely changed. What changed is how much work the user has to do to understand it.

shiftlyn.com/results
V1 · the free state — everything before ₹10
  • 1
    The emotional payoff is never gated

    Score, gauge, risk verdict and the full skill-by-skill reasoning are free. Users leave knowing where they stand even if they never pay.

  • 2
    The diagnosis is free, the plan is paid

    What money buys is the roadmap — quick wins, pivot paths, live jobs, courses. The line sits between knowing and doing.

  • 3
    One skill shown, the rest blurred

    Blur rather than absence — the shape of what's behind the wall is visible, so the ask lands as a preview instead of a wall.

  • 4
    The ask is itemized and one-time

    "₹199 one-time · no subscription · instant access", with the seven things included listed above it. Nothing about the price needs interpreting.

shiftlyn.com/results
V2 · the first full report — what testing broke
  • 1
    The reasoning was prose

    "Why your score is 62" ran as three paragraphs. Users skimmed them, then asked the question the paragraphs had just answered.

  • 2
    Two systems for one number

    A full rainbow arc and a separate Safe / Moderate / Risk legend underneath — the reader had to reconcile them.

  • 3
    No per-skill view

    Skills to learn were listed, but nothing showed how exposed each existing skill already was — the part users most wanted.

  • 4
    Everything at one weight

    Sections were evenly sized and evenly toned, so nothing indicated where to start reading or acting.

shiftlyn.com/results
V3 · current — same data, restructured
  • 1
    Paragraphs became a table

    Your skill × how much AI can already do × why — eight rows the user can scan in the time it took to read one paragraph.

  • 2
    Exposure reads at a glance

    A LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH chip per skill turns the judgement into a pattern instead of a paragraph.

  • 3
    The verdict is stated in words

    A "High risk — act soon" pill sits above the number, so the score's meaning never depends on reading the colour correctly.

  • 4
    Advice carries its evidence

    Each skill is ranked into Level 1 or Level 2 with a timeline and a cited source — the recommendation stops being an opinion.

Results Page — V1 → V3Same assessment, three treatments. Switch between versions to compare the free state, the first report, and the current one.

What actually improved

Visual hierarchy Verdict, evidence, then plan — each step visibly smaller than the one before it.
A calmer gauge One hue instead of four, zones labelled in place, and the risk level said out loud.
Feedback on every action Section tabs swap in place, skills expand without a reload, and saving a course confirms itself.
Skills as a table Prose became rows: the skill, how much AI can do today, and the reason why.

2. Choosing the right login pattern

Login and onboarding aren't one-size-fits-all — what works for a social app fails for a one-time assessment tool. We initially gated the report behind a standard email OTP, the default most teams reach for. It was the wrong call.

Our users had already paid — every extra step between payment and the report was another chance to lose them. So we compared the realistic options for an Indian audience on one question: what's most likely to fail between "user pays" and "user sees their report"?

Login method How it works Drop-off risk Reliability Fits the post-payment gate?
Email OTPcurrent Code emailed, user types it High — every slow, spam, or dropped email is a stuck paying user Weak — email deliverability is the #1 silent failure ✗ Worst fit as a gate — fine only as a return-login option
Magic link Click a link in an email to log in High — same email dependency as OTP Weak, same as above ✗ Same flaw — don't gate the report on it
Phone / SMS OTP Code sent via SMS to phone Low–medium — SMS lands reliably in India; cost per message Strong in India, much better than email ◐ Good for return logins — not as the buy-gate
WhatsApp OTP Code via WhatsApp Business API Low — Indians live on WhatsApp, near-instant Very strong in India ◐ Great fallback channel — needs WhatsApp Business setup
Continue with Google / Apple OAuth — no code sent at all Very low — one tap, nothing to deliver Strongest — no email or SMS to fail ✓ Excellent — kills the deliverability problem entirely
Password User sets and types a password Medium — friction plus reset-flow pain Strong — no delivery dependency ◐ Fine as fallback credential — not primary
Order-token access The payment success page itself unlocks the report using the order ID Lowest — they're already on the page Strongest — independent of any message ✓✓ Best immediate unlock — this is the real fix
Login Method Comparison — India, Post-Payment Context
The fix

The payment success page now unlocks the report directly with the order ID — zero deliverability risk, because nothing has to be sent anywhere. Google/Apple sign-in became the primary path for return visits, with phone and WhatsApp OTP kept as fallbacks for an audience that trusts SMS and WhatsApp far more than email. Email OTP and magic links were demoted from gate to optional.

12 — Key Insights

What we heard,
again and again.

Testing told us whether the product worked. Living with it told us something harder: which problems were about the interface, and which were about the product itself. Four themes kept surfacing — and none were fixable by moving something on a screen.

How many runs do I get for this price?

That question came up more than any other. We had priced a report while users were trying to work out whether they were buying a tool — and no amount of layout work answers that.

Seniority caps the value Senior users already knew most of what we recommended. The advice has to get deeper with experience, or the product has an audience ceiling.
Pricing was the wrong unit "How many runs for this price?" is a question about the model, not the number. One-time result or ongoing access — the answer had to be visible before checkout.
No consistent identity A product asking people to trust a verdict about their career can't look improvised. Branding stopped being cosmetic once trust became the conversion problem.
People wanted people Events, networking, exposure, support. Career anxiety is social, and a private score answers only half of it — which is what pushed communities into the dashboard.
Key Insights — Beyond the InterfaceFour themes that usability fixes couldn't reach: audience depth, pricing model, identity, and the need for other people.
13 — Retention Challenges

Why should users
come back?

Shiftlyn has a structural retention problem, and it isn't attention span. Duolingo — one of the products we took apart early on — has a daily loop built into what it teaches. An AI readiness check doesn't. Once you know your score, the product has done its job.

So the return visit can't come from the score. It has to come from things that keep moving on their own — the market, the roles, the skills — while the user's report sits still.

Retention opportunities

Four kinds of value keep changing after the report is delivered, and each one is a reason to reopen it. These are what the dashboard was eventually built around.

AI risk updates Fresh signal as roles and tasks shift.
Job notifications Personalized to role and risk profile.
Course recommendations Matched to the skills that matter next.
Skill tracking Visible progress over time, not just a score.

14 — The Long-Term Vision

A one-stop platform
for AI upskilling.

The assessment was never meant to be the whole product — it was the cheapest way to prove people wanted the thing behind it. What that thing became is a single place to answer one recurring question: am I still safe, and what do I do about it?

Answering it properly takes three things, and only the first is a test.

Know where you stand AI readiness measured against your actual role and tasks, with the skill gaps named rather than implied. This is the part that ships today.
Close the gap Relevant skills, matched courses, and the roles they open up — with progress tracked so effort is visible instead of remembered.
Stay ahead of it Industry movement and professional networks — because the risk score changes when the market does, not when the user asks.
Long-Term Vision — Three JobsMeasure, close, and keep watching. The assessment is only the first of the three.
15 — Business Constraints

Optimizing cost
without losing quality.

Every report is generated, not retrieved. That's what makes the output specific enough to be worth paying for — and it's also the reason this product has a cost problem that a course library doesn't: each result burns tokens before it earns a rupee.

At a ₹10 one-time price, the margin on a single report is thin by design. Costs stack up across the Claude API, Azure infrastructure, MongoDB, setup, marketing — but the only one that grows with every user is tokens per result. Getting that number down was the difference between a product that scales and a demo that gets expensive as it succeeds.

Solution — route by difficulty, not by default

Not every step of a report needs the strongest model. We split the work: Gemini handles selected workloads, Claude is reserved for the advanced analysis where the reasoning quality is what the user is actually paying for.

16 — Impact & Learnings

Six lessons, in the order
they cost the most.

We set out to build an assessment. What we ended up defending, version after version, was the twenty seconds after the score appears — which is why that one screen was rewritten three times while the questions behind it barely changed.

The quiz was never the product. That, and five other things I'd carry into the next one.

The assessment is data collection. The results are the product.

Everything users valued lived after the quiz, not inside it. We spent early effort tuning questions when the leverage was entirely on the page that reported them.

Structure beats prose when someone is anxious.

The same reasoning, moved from three paragraphs into a skill-by-skill table, stopped users asking the question it had already answered. Worried people scan; they don't read.

Put the paywall after the payoff, not in front of it.

Score, verdict and reasoning stay free; the plan is what's paid. Asking before the user feels anything converts worse and reads worse.

Specificity is the whole value.

Generic advice reads as filler no matter how well it's designed. Every gain came from making the output more particular to one person's role, tasks and experience.

Pick the audience the product is actually good for.

Senior users already knew most of what we recommended. Students and early-career professionals were the ones who acted on it — and naming that made every other decision easier.

Retention has to be designed, not hoped for.

A one-time score has no natural reason to be revisited. The return visit had to be built — from things that keep changing on their own — or it doesn't happen.

17 — Conclusion

Clarity is the product,
not the content.

Shiftlyn started as a five-minute AI readiness check and became something harder to describe: a place to find out what AI means for your particular job, and what to do about it this month.

The single most useful thing the MVP taught us is that nobody is short of learning material. Our users already had more courses than they could finish. What they didn't have was clarity — where they stand today, what's genuinely at risk, and which one action moves them forward next. Content was never the scarce resource. Judgement was.

Great products don't just provide information. They help people make better decisions.

It's still in progress — pricing, retention and depth for experienced users are all open. But the problem is the right one. In a market where careers are being rewritten faster than people can react, helping someone decide what to do next is worth more than handing them another library.
